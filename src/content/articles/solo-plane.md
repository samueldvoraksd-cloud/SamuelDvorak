Most student pilots want their first solo as soon as possible, and that goal is earlier reached if you approach training the right way.

Your first lessons should build the fundamentals of basic attitude flying: straight-and-level flight, turns, climbs, and descents. 

Once you have a solid feel for the airplane and understand basic attitude flying, your lessons move into more specific maneuvers: slow flight, stalls, ground-reference work, and the other exercises your training requires.

Basic attitude flying is the foundation for everything else you'll do in the air. Learn it well before moving on, because every maneuver that follows builds on those same skills.

Landing practice comes next, and it takes far more than learning to touch down on a runway. You need to taxi the airplane, make the right radio calls, hold altitude, understand the traffic pattern, manage airspeed and power, and recognize the correct sight picture, among other things.

I see many students start their first landing lessons without studying the basics first. They don't know what a traffic pattern is, what radio calls they'll need to make, or what "pitch for airspeed, power for altitude" actually means. That puts extra workload on the student that a little preparation would have avoided.

If I had to give one piece of advice to a student chasing a solo, it's this: take initiative, and stay hungry to learn. Your instructor teaches, demonstrates, coaches, and evaluates you in the airplane, but how fast you improve depends just as much on what you do at home. Open the book, study the procedures, learn the terminology, and review what happened in your last lesson. Figure out what you struggled with and why, then come back to the next lesson ready to apply it.

None of this requires spending a fortune on books. The FAA publishes aviation handbooks for free, and they're worth knowing well: the Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, the Airplane Flying Handbook, and the Aviation Weather Handbook. The Pilot's Handbook covers the academic side of flying — aerodynamics, aircraft systems, weather, airport operations, airspace, navigation, and aeromedical factors. The Airplane Flying Handbook covers the practical side: basic maneuvers, takeoffs and landings, traffic patterns, ground-reference work, and emergency procedures. The Aviation Weather Handbook builds your understanding of weather and how it affects flying.

You don't need to read all three cover to cover before your first flight. Use them as references throughout training instead. Before a lesson on traffic patterns, read up on traffic patterns and landings. Before you practice stalls, review the relevant section of the Airplane Flying Handbook. Show up to each lesson with a basic understanding of what you're about to do, not a memorized textbook.

An airplane is one of the worst places to learn something for the first time. It's loud, distracting, and demands your full attention, which is why it works best as the place where you apply what you already learned on the ground. Some things can only be learned there: how much control input a takeoff needs, how much rudder pressure different phases of flight require, what a proper flare feels like, and plenty of other skills no textbook can fully teach. But walking into your lessons already understanding the fundamentals changes how much time you spend flying instead of catching up on things you could have studied at home.

Before your first landing lessons, get familiar with how a traffic pattern works and why it exists. You'll likely have little experience making radio calls in the pattern by the time you start practicing landings, and that's fine as long as you've done a little study to give you the edge. Things like knowing and  understand the basic structure of a call can go a long way:

Who you're talking to → who you are → where you are → what information you have → what you want to do.

An initial call to ground might sound like: "Easterwood Ground, Skyhawk 8821U is at the south ramp with information Zulu, remaining in the pattern." A radio call sounds intimidating the first time you hear it, but once you know the building blocks, constructing your own gets much easier. The same principle applies to nearly every part of flight training: the more you understand before you get in the airplane, the more mental capacity you have left to fly it.

If your goal is soloing in the minimum safe time for you, count preparation, not flight hours. Study on the ground, apply it in the air, and ask questions when you're unsure. Your instructor teaches you to fly; your commitment to learning outside the airplane decides how fast you get there.
